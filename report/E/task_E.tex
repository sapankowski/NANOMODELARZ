\subsection{Purpose}

Task E calculates the linear mechanical properties of the relaxed magnetic
ground-state structures identified in Task B using finite-difference elastic
tensors in VASP.  For Ni, the calculation uses the relaxed ferromagnetic PBE
structure.  For NiO, the calculation uses the relaxed DFT+\(U\) AFM-II
structure.  These elastic-tensor calculations provide the second-order elastic
constants and derived mechanical properties including bulk modulus, shear
modulus, Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, and mechanical stability criteria.

\subsection{Computational Setup and Assumptions}

The mechanical properties were computed from VASP finite-difference elastic
tensors using \texttt{IBRION = 6}, \texttt{ISIF = 3}, \texttt{NFREE = 2}, and a
small strain step \texttt{POTIM = 0.015}; this is the VASP finite-difference
elastic-constant approach for strained cells with stress evaluation enabled
\cite{vasp-ibrion,vasp-isif}.  The calculations used the same relaxed
ground-state structures and magnetic configurations selected in Task B:
ferromagnetic PBE Ni and AFM-II DFT+\(U\) NiO.  For NiO, the two Ni sites were
kept as formal \texttt{Ni\_up} and \texttt{Ni\_down} species with duplicated Ni
PAW datasets so that the opposite magnetic moments remain explicit during the
elastic-tensor calculation \cite{vasp-nio-u}.

Both elastic-tensor calculations used the same k-point mesh densities as the
parent Task B calculations to ensure consistent electronic structure sampling.
For Ni, the ferromagnetic ground state was used without changes in magnetic
configuration.  For NiO, the antiferromagnetic AFM-II ordering was maintained
throughout the finite-displacement cycle.

The full elastic tensor was reduced to effective cubic constants by averaging
the equivalent components,
\[
C_{11} = \langle C_{xx,xx}, C_{yy,yy}, C_{zz,zz}\rangle,\qquad
C_{12} = \langle C_{xx,yy}, C_{xx,zz}, C_{yy,xx}, C_{yy,zz}, C_{zz,xx},
C_{zz,yy}\rangle,
\]
and
\[
C_{44} = \langle C_{xy,xy}, C_{yz,yz}, C_{zx,zx}\rangle.
\]
The bulk modulus was evaluated as \(B=(C_{11}+2C_{12})/3\).  The shear modulus
reported in the table is the Voigt--Reuss--Hill average for a cubic material
\cite{voigt,reuss,hill}, which was then used to compute Young's modulus and
Poisson's ratio:
\[
E = \frac{9BG}{3B+G},\qquad
\nu = \frac{3B-2G}{2(3B+G)}.
\]
A cubic material is mechanically stable when the Born stability conditions
\(C_{11}-C_{12}>0\), \(C_{11}+2C_{12}>0\), and \(C_{44}>0\) are satisfied
\cite{born-huang,mouhat-coudert}.  These criteria ensure that the elastic
energy is positive for small homogeneous strains around the equilibrium
structure.

\subsection{Discussion}

Both calculated structures satisfy the cubic mechanical-stability criteria.  The
bulk moduli are relatively close (\(B_{\mathrm{Ni}} = 196~\mathrm{GPa}\) and
\(B_{\mathrm{NiO}} = 184~\mathrm{GPa}\)), suggesting comparable
incompressibilities.  However, NiO has a slightly higher shear modulus
(\(G_{\mathrm{NiO}} = 102~\mathrm{GPa}\) vs.\ \(G_{\mathrm{Ni}} = 95~\mathrm{GPa}\)),
making it somewhat stiffer against shear deformations.

The Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio are useful engineering parameters for
small deformations.  Both materials show positive Poisson ratios in the range
0.27--0.29, which is typical for cubic and rocksalt systems.  The higher Young's
modulus of NiO (\(E = 259~\mathrm{GPa}\) vs.\ \(E = 245~\mathrm{GPa}\) for Ni)
is consistent with the somewhat stiffer shear response of the ionic oxide.
At the same time, the bulk modulus is not larger for NiO in this calculation,
so the result should be interpreted more specifically: NiO resists shape change
slightly more strongly, while Ni is marginally less compressible.

The three independent cubic constants also give useful information beyond the
averaged moduli.  The large \(C_{11}\) values in both materials show strong
resistance to uniaxial strain.  Ni has the larger \(C_{12}\), which is why its
bulk modulus is slightly higher despite having the smaller \(C_{11}\).  The
Zener anisotropy factor \(A=2C_{44}/(C_{11}-C_{12})\) is about \(2.20\) for Ni
and \(0.85\) for NiO, so neither material is perfectly elastically isotropic in
the cubic approximation.  This is normal for single crystals; the
Voigt--Reuss--Hill values reported above are therefore best understood as
polycrystalline averages derived from the single-crystal elastic tensor.

Compared with experimental and tabulated single-crystal data, the elastic
constants have the right order of magnitude but should not be overinterpreted.
For Ni, room-temperature handbooks give roughly \(C_{11}=247~\mathrm{GPa}\),
\(C_{12}=147~\mathrm{GPa}\), and \(C_{44}=125~\mathrm{GPa}\)
\cite{simmons-wang}.  The present PBE values are slightly higher, especially
\(C_{11}\), which is consistent with comparing a static 0 K DFT calculation
with finite-temperature experimental elastic constants.  For NiO, literature
values are more sensitive to temperature, magnetic order, and the treatment of
correlation, but tabulated and theoretical results place \(C_{11}\) in the
few-hundred-GPa range and \(C_{12}\), \(C_{44}\) near
\(10^2~\mathrm{GPa}\) \cite{simmons-wang,dudarev,mp-nio}.  The calculated
DFT+\(U\) NiO tensor is therefore physically plausible, although the
\(C_{11}\) value should be checked with larger-cell and stricter magnetic
convergence tests before being quoted as a quantitative prediction.

The elastic constant tensor for Ni shows the expected cubic symmetry with
negligible off-diagonal components, confirming that the relaxed ferromagnetic
PBE structure is a well-ordered cubic crystal.  The NiO elastic tensor also
displays approximate cubic symmetry in the diagonal block but shows small
off-diagonal components ($< 0.5~\mathrm{GPa}$), which are likely due to
numerical noise or minor symmetry breaking in the AFM-II magnetic ordering.

These results establish that both relaxed structures are mechanically stable
according to linear elasticity theory, supporting the ground-state geometries
determined in Task B as realistic equilibrium configurations.  The elastic
constants can also be used to estimate long-wavelength acoustic response and as
input to continuum-mechanics simulations.
